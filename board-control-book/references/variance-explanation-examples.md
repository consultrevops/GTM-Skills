# Variance Explanation Examples

Section 6 of the control book requires four parts for every variance above threshold: what happened, why it happened, what's changing, and what it means for next period.

This file shows the difference between an explanation that survives a follow-up question and one that doesn't. Each example is written twice.

---

## What Makes a Variance Explanation Fail

Most bad explanations fail in one of four ways.

**Category instead of mechanism.** "Macro headwinds" names a category of cause without saying which specific thing happened to which specific deals. A board can't act on a category.

**Passive construction hiding the actor.** "Deals were delayed" invites the question nobody asks in the room but everyone thinks: delayed by whom, and why.

**A fix with no owner or date.** "We're addressing it" is not a plan. It's an acknowledgment.

**No forward implication.** An explanation that stops at what happened leaves the board to work out the consequence themselves, usually less accurately than you would have.

## Example 1: Win Rate Decline

### Weak version

> **What happened:** Win rates declined in Q2.
>
> **Why it happened:** Increased competitive pressure and longer buying cycles in the current macro environment.
>
> **What's changing:** The team is focused on improving competitive positioning.
>
> **What it means:** We expect improvement in Q3.

**What's wrong with it:** "Increased competitive pressure" is a category, not a mechanism. Nobody named a competitor, a segment, or a deal. "The team is focused on" has no owner and no date. "We expect improvement" is a hope stated as a forecast. A board member's first question will be "which competitor," and there's no answer prepared.

### Strong version

> **What happened:** Enterprise win rate dropped 8 points, from 34% in Q1 to 26% in Q2. Mid-market and SMB win rates were flat.
>
> **Why it happened:** Five enterprise losses drove the decline. Three were deals where our champion changed roles mid-cycle and the successor restarted evaluation. Two were competitive losses to [Competitor] on price, both in the financial services vertical where they released a compliance module in April that we don't currently match. No other segment showed a pattern.
>
> **What's changing:** A multi-threading requirement was added to the enterprise deal review process in week 3 of Q2, requiring a documented second contact at director level or above before a deal can advance past Stage 3. Sarah Chen owns this. On the competitive side, product has scoped the compliance gap and will present a build-versus-partner recommendation by September 15.
>
> **What it means for next period:** Enterprise pipeline coverage is 2.8x against a 3.5x target. If champion turnover continues at the Q2 rate, we should discount the enterprise forecast by approximately 12%. The competitive gap in financial services affects roughly $2.1M of current pipeline, which we've moved from Commit to Best Case pending the product decision.

**Why it works:** Every claim is checkable. The board can ask which three deals, which competitor, which vertical, and there's an answer. The fix has an owner and a date. The forward implication is specific enough to act on, including a stated pipeline reclassification.

## Example 2: Churn Spike

### Weak version

> **What happened:** Churn was higher than plan this quarter.
>
> **Why it happened:** A few larger accounts didn't renew. Some were impacted by budget constraints on their side.
>
> **What's changing:** CS is increasing touchpoints with at-risk accounts.
>
> **What it means:** We're watching retention closely.

**What's wrong with it:** "A few larger accounts" avoids naming a number. "Some were impacted by budget constraints" attributes the loss to the customer's situation, which is comfortable but usually incomplete. "Increasing touchpoints" is activity, not a mechanism. "Watching closely" commits to nothing.

### Strong version

> **What happened:** Gross revenue retention came in at 84% against a 91% plan. Seven accounts churned, representing $840K in ARR. Four of the seven were above $100K ARR.
>
> **Why it happened:** Six of the seven were on the legacy pricing model and hit a renewal quote 40% above their prior rate, driven by the pricing change we implemented in October. Their usage had not grown proportionally. The seventh was an acquisition where the acquirer consolidated onto their existing vendor. Health scores flagged four of the seven as at-risk more than 90 days before renewal, but none had an executive engagement logged in that window.
>
> **What's changing:** Two things. First, we've built a legacy-pricing renewal list of 34 remaining accounts and are running proactive value reviews starting this month, owned by Marcus Reid, with all 34 covered by October 30. Second, we've added a rule that any account flagged at-risk 90 days out requires a logged executive touch, enforced in the CS workflow as of August 1.
>
> **What it means for next period:** Of the 34 legacy-pricing accounts, 11 renew in Q3 representing $1.2M ARR. We are forecasting 75% retention on that cohort against 91% on the rest of the base, which puts Q3 GRR at approximately 87%. The renewal pricing approach is under review with finance, and a recommendation will come to the board in the Q4 cycle.

**Why it works:** It names the real cause, which is an internal pricing decision, rather than the more comfortable one, which is customer budget. It also surfaces a process failure (health scores fired, nobody acted) rather than hiding it. The forward number is specific and cohort-based rather than a blended guess.

## Example 3: Pipeline Coverage Shortfall

### Weak version

> **What happened:** Pipeline coverage is below target.
>
> **Why it happened:** Lead volume was lower than expected and the sales team was focused on closing Q2 deals.
>
> **What's changing:** Marketing is increasing top-of-funnel investment and we're adding SDR capacity.
>
> **What it means:** We should recover coverage over the next quarter.

**What's wrong with it:** Two causes are named but neither is quantified, so nobody can tell which one matters more. "Increasing investment" and "adding capacity" have no numbers, owners, or dates. The forward statement is a hope with no mechanism behind it.

### Strong version

> **What happened:** Pipeline coverage for Q3 is 2.4x against a 3.0x target, a shortfall of roughly $4.8M in qualified pipeline.
>
> **Why it happened:** Two contributing factors, weighted. Roughly 70% of the gap traces to a 34% drop in inbound MQLs beginning in May, which coincided with the website migration on May 12. Form tracking broke on four landing pages and was not detected for 19 days, so an estimated 340 leads were lost entirely. The remaining 30% is normal end-of-quarter pipeline generation slowdown, consistent with the same period in the prior two years.
>
> **What's changing:** Form tracking was restored May 31 and monitoring alerts were added June 4, owned by Priya Nair. The 340 lost leads cannot be recovered, but a reactivation campaign to the affected page visitors launched July 8 and has generated 41 MQLs to date. For the structural piece, we are not adding SDR capacity this quarter. The gap is a lead-volume problem, not a coverage problem, and adding SDRs would not have prevented it.
>
> **What it means for next period:** July MQL volume has recovered to 96% of the pre-migration baseline. If August and September hold, Q4 coverage returns to target without intervention. If July volume proves to be a partial recovery rather than a full one, we will need approximately $180K in incremental demand generation spend to close the Q4 gap, and we will bring that request to the board in the October cycle rather than acting on it now.

**Why it works:** The two causes are weighted rather than just listed, so the board knows where to focus. It explicitly rejects an intuitive fix (adding SDRs) with a reason, which is more credible than accepting it. And it names a conditional future ask with a trigger, rather than either hiding a coming spend request or asking for it prematurely.

## Example 4: A Variance You Can't Fully Explain Yet

Sometimes the quarter closes before the analysis does. This is the version that maintains credibility.

### Weak version

> **What happened:** Deal velocity slowed in mid-market.
>
> **Why it happened:** We're still investigating, but it may be related to the new pricing structure or possibly increased competitive activity in the segment.
>
> **What's changing:** We'll have a full analysis next quarter.
>
> **What it means:** Too early to say.

**What's wrong with it:** "May be related to X or possibly Y" is guessing out loud. Offering two unverified causes is worse than offering none, because the board will remember one of them as the explanation. And "too early to say" abdicates the forward-looking part entirely.

### Strong version

> **What happened:** Mid-market average days-to-close increased from 47 to 61 days between Q1 and Q2, a 30% slowdown. Enterprise and SMB velocity were unchanged.
>
> **Why it happened:** We do not have a confirmed cause yet. What we can verify: the slowdown is concentrated in the Stage 3 to Stage 4 transition, which moved from an average of 12 days to 24 days. It began in the second week of April. It affects all three mid-market territories roughly equally, which rules out a single rep or manager as the driver. Two hypotheses remain open. The first is the pricing structure change that took effect April 1, which added an approval step for discounts above 15%. The second is a change in deal mix toward accounts with more stakeholders. We are pulling approval-step timestamps and stakeholder counts to test both, with results by August 22, owned by Dan Whitfield.
>
> **What's changing:** Nothing yet. We are not adjusting the process until we know which hypothesis holds, because the two fixes point in opposite directions.
>
> **What it means for next period:** If the slowdown persists at the current rate, roughly $900K of Q3 mid-market pipeline slips to Q4. We have held the Q3 forecast at the original number rather than discounting it, because two of the three affected territories showed velocity recovery in the last three weeks of the quarter. We will update the board with the analysis result before the Q3 close, not at the next scheduled cycle.

**Why it works:** It separates what is verified from what is hypothesized, and labels each. It explains why no action has been taken, which is more credible than performing activity for the board's benefit. It commits to a date and an owner for the answer. And it still gives a forward number with the reasoning behind it, rather than hiding behind uncertainty.

## Example 5: A Variance in Your Favor

Positive variances need explanations too. An unexplained beat is as much of a gap as an unexplained miss, and it's the one teams most often skip.

### Weak version

> **What happened:** We exceeded the new ARR plan by 18%.
>
> **Why it happened:** Strong execution from the sales team and good market conditions.
>
> **What's changing:** Nothing, we're continuing what's working.
>
> **What it means:** We're raising the Q3 target.

**What's wrong with it:** "Strong execution" is a compliment, not an explanation. If you can't say why you beat plan, you can't say whether it will repeat, which makes raising the target a guess. Boards are as suspicious of unexplained beats as unexplained misses, because both mean nobody understands the mechanism.

### Strong version

> **What happened:** New ARR came in at $3.4M against a $2.9M plan, an 18% beat.
>
> **Why it happened:** $380K of the $500K variance traces to two deals that were forecast for Q3 and closed early, both in the enterprise segment, both pulled forward by the customer's own fiscal year timing rather than by anything we did. The remaining $120K is genuine outperformance, distributed across mid-market with no single driver. Average deal size in mid-market rose 9%, which we believe is attributable to the packaging change in February, though we have not isolated it from segment mix effects.
>
> **What's changing:** Nothing operationally. We are flagging the pull-forward effect in the Q3 forecast so the comparison is understood.
>
> **What it means for next period:** Q3 new ARR should be read against a plan that is effectively $380K lighter than it appears, since those deals have already closed. We are not raising the Q3 target. The underlying run rate improved by roughly 4%, not 18%, and we would rather set the target against the real number.

**Why it works:** It separates timing from performance, which is the single most important distinction in a positive variance. It resists the temptation to claim credit for a customer's fiscal calendar. And it declines to raise the target, with a reason, which builds far more credibility than accepting the beat at face value.

## Checklist for Every Variance Explanation

Before a variance narrative goes into the deck:

- [ ] The number is stated with both the actual and the comparison point
- [ ] The cause names specific deals, segments, accounts, or events, not a category
- [ ] Where multiple causes exist, they are weighted rather than just listed
- [ ] Any AI-generated causal analysis passed the why-audit before inclusion
- [ ] Verified facts are separated from hypotheses, and each is labeled
- [ ] The fix has a named owner and a date
- [ ] If no fix is planned, the reason is stated
- [ ] The forward implication includes a number, not just a direction
- [ ] Positive variances are explained with the same rigor as negative ones
- [ ] No phrase from this list appears anywhere: macro headwinds, timing dynamics, pipeline softness, market conditions, execution challenges, strong execution, we're monitoring closely

---

## The Underlying Principle

A board can absorb a bad quarter. What erodes trust is an explanation that falls apart under one follow-up question.

Every one of the strong examples above would survive a director asking "which deals," "who owns that," "how do you know," or "what if it doesn't recover." That's the actual test. Write the explanation, then ask those four questions of it yourself, and fix whatever it can't answer.
